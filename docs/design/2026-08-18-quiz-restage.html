Three things going wrong

  • The action is buried inside a container. A green button on a white card on a stone field is three surfaces deep. Nothing on the screen is louder than it — but nothing is quieter either, so it has to be found rather than seen.
  • The card argues with the list for the same job. Both are white rounded rectangles with headings and hairlines. A first-time reader gets no signal that one is a thing to do and the other is a thing to look up.
  • Warm ground, cold furniture. The field is warm stone, and then every object placed on it is pure white with grey type. The one warm colour the palette owns — clay — appears as a 6pt bar and a few 7pt dots.

Three restagings

All three keep the twenty rows, the tick, the clay dot and the count. They differ in what the top of the screen is.

Where the warmth actually comes from

Not from adding a colour. Every value below is already in src/ui/theme.ts — what changes is how much of the screen they are allowed to cover.

  • One saturated object, not one saturated button. A 46pt button is about 8% of the first screen. The plank is closer to 20%, and it is the only place the accent appears on the tab — so the screen reads as warm rather than as white with a green detail.
  • The progress bar comes out of the card. Under the title, full width, it stops being decoration on a control and becomes the line that says how you are doing. That is the clay role, stated plainly.
  • Copy does as much work as colour. “Practise” is a category; “Start a round — ten questions, about two minutes” is an invitation with the cost printed on it. “Look them up” says what the list below is for, which nothing currently does.

One tension worth naming

The plank is green, and what the screen reports is yours

The palette splits the two deliberately: green is the road — go, route, correct — and clay is you. A green plank keeps that rule, because starting a round is a go. But it does put the two warm colours ten points apart on one screen for the first time in the app.

A keeps them in separate registers so they do not fight: clay is a 6pt line of information under the title, green is a solid object you press. B does the opposite and nests the green inside the clay — that is the thing to look at hardest in B’s mockup above.

What shipped, and where it departs from the mockup

B, built. Three things came out differently once it met the code, each deliberate.

  • The button still says “Start the quiz”, not “Start a round”. The mockup had the latter, but the tab already carries a test named the button names the thing it starts, not a word only this app uses — “round” is jargon this app invented. That earlier decision wins over a fresh mockup, and the sentence inside the hero was reworded off the same word.
  • The clay ground needed three colours the palette did not have. This is the first surface in the app that is neither the cell nor the field, and bare clay measures 3.54 against its own tint — fine for the 6pt bar, not fine for the sentence beside it. So clayInk, clayInkSoft and clayTrack were added as roles, with the contrast floor written into theme.test.ts the same way the accent’s already is. Reusing clay as ink would have shipped unreadable type on the one screen that uses it.
  • The status line is two nodes, not one string. “9 done” takes the heavier ink because it is the half that is about you; “11 to go” sits back. Nested text concatenates, so a separator inside the same string becomes part of the words each half is read as — which is a screen reader problem before it is a test problem.
