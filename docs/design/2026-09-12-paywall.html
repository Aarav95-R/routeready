One gate, nine routes behind it

The split

What's free, what's paid

The free route is complete — turn-by-turn navigation, the hazards, the examiner, the mark sheet, the verdict. A learner can drive it, be marked on it, and see exactly what the app is for without paying anything. That is deliberate: the product sells itself far better than a feature list does.

Why two prices, and why these two

£3 a week works out at £13 a month. £9 a month works out at £2.08 a week. The weekly option is not really there to be chosen — it's there to make the monthly one look obvious, and it does that arithmetic for the reader the moment both are on screen.

It also earns its place honestly. A learner whose test is on Thursday genuinely only needs six days, and charging them £9 for it would be the wrong answer. The sign-up interview already knows which of the two they are — testWindow is captured on card 2 — so the sheet can pre-select the right one rather than making them work it out.

The gate

Nine locked cards do the selling

A learner opens their home centre and sees ten route cards. One is open. Nine are not. That list is the paywall — it arrives at the exact moment someone has decided to practise, which is the highest-intent moment the app has.

The important call here: locked cards keep their route drawing. The thumbnail is the argument — nine real, distinct routes around the centre they're about to be tested at. Withholding it would turn a shop window into a shuttered one. What the server actually withholds is the drivable part: the turn-by-turn steps, the speed envelope and the hazards. You cannot drive a route from a 150-pixel squiggle, so the sell and the security don't conflict.

The observation zones stay public with the geometry, which looks like a mistake and isn't: they feed the card as well as the drive. plainSummary() tallies them to write the locked card's second line — the "dual carriageway, box junction" that is doing the selling — and the thumbnail draws a pin for each. Gating them would empty the exact sentence the locked card exists for, while withholding nothing anyone could drive from.

The paywall sheet A @gorhom/bottom-sheet, which the app already uses for the examiner panel — so it's a familiar object, not a new kind of screen. The headline names the centre they were just looking at. The interview's answer to "when is your test" picks the pre-selected price and writes the second line.

Three rules the sheet follows

  • It names the centre. "Nine more routes at Wood Green" outperforms "Upgrade to Premium" because it describes something the person was already trying to do.
  • No second green. Per the palette rules, green stays the route-and-action colour — it's on the CTA and the free route's drawing, nowhere else on the sheet. Clay carries the personal line about the test date. The locked state is neutral stone, not red: a locked route isn't an error.
  • It can always be dismissed. No full-screen takeover, no delayed close button. The sheet is a door, not a wall — and App Review treats it that way too.

Placement

Where the sheet is allowed to appear

Ranked by how much intent the person has already shown. The top two carry the conversion; the rest exist so nobody hits a dead end.

Highest
Tapping a locked route card

They picked a specific route at a specific centre. This is the moment — the sheet opens with that route's name and drawing still visible behind it.

High
The panel under the route list

"9 more routes here." Browsing rather than committing, but still on the centre screen with intent. One tap to the same sheet.

Medium
After a mock test verdict on the free route

They've just been marked and told what to work on. The result screen's next step should point at a route that drills the leading fault — and that route is usually locked. Worth doing, worth doing gently: this lands on someone who just failed something.

Medium
Home's next-step card

nextStep() in homeView.ts already computes what to practise from the fault record. When its recommendation is a locked route, the card says so plainly rather than sending them to a wall.

Low
Settings

A subscription row: status, renewal date, manage, and restore. Not a sales surface — App Review requires restore to be findable, and a lapsed subscriber needs somewhere to go.

And where it is not allowed

Never during a drive. Never on launch. Never over the quiz or the manoeuvres. Never more than once per screen visit. A learner mid-mock-test who loses their subscription finishes the mock test — more on that below.

Architecture

How it's wired

RevenueCat wraps StoreKit and hands you receipt validation, renewals, billing retries, refunds and cross-device restore. It's free below $2.5k a month. With a weekly product you'd otherwise be writing 52 renewal events a year of edge-case handling yourself.

The truth about who has paid lives in Supabase, written by a RevenueCat webhook, and is enforced by row-level security — not by the client. The app reads it, but the app is never what decides.

The data changes

Three of them, and the middle one is the load-bearing idea.

  • routes.is_free — a boolean, hand-picked one per centre rather than "whichever sorts first". The free route is the demo, so it should be a representative mid-difficulty one, not an alphabetical accident.
  • A new route_details table holding steps and speed_profile, moved off routes. This is what makes column-level gating possible at all: RLS is row-level, so the fields that must be withheld have to live in rows of their own. routes keeps name, geometry, observation zones, difficulty, distance and duration — everything a card draws, all public.
  • An entitlements table, one row per account, written only by the webhook's service role and readable only by its owner. Plus an is_subscribed() SQL helper that both the route_details and hazards policies call.

On the client

A useEntitlement store alongside the existing sessionStore, holding active | lapsed | unknown. It mirrors RevenueCat's cached customer info to AsyncStorage so a learner with no signal still opens the app entitled, and it exposes one selector the screens actually use: canDrive(route).

Risks found in the code

Three things that will bite

These came out of reading the existing services rather than from a checklist. Each one is cheap to handle now and expensive to discover in TestFlight.

Cached routes survive a lapsed subscription
src/services/cache.ts

cachedFetch writes every route list to AsyncStorage under cache:routes:<centreId>, and clearUserCache() deliberately sweeps only cache:progress: — its comment explains why: routes "belong to the country, not the person." That reasoning is correct today and stops being correct the moment routes are entitlement-scoped. Without a change, someone subscribes, caches nine routes, cancels, and keeps driving them offline indefinitely.

Fix Sweep cache:route keys when entitlement drops to lapsed, and update that comment — it encodes an assumption this feature invalidates, and leaving it there will mislead whoever reads it next.

The route list ships everything it has
src/services/routesRepo.ts

Both listRoutesByCentre and getRoute call select("*"). A gate built only in the UI would leave the full turn-by-turn payload sitting in the response of an unlocked query — bypassable by anyone who opens a proxy, and quietly cached to disk besides.

Fix The route_details split above, plus naming the columns explicitly instead of *. Then the gate is enforced by Postgres and the client is merely reflecting it — which is the only arrangement worth trusting.

A subscription can lapse mid-drive
app/route/[id]/mock.tsx

A mock test runs for twenty-odd minutes with the screen awake and location streaming. If a renewal fails or a token refresh returns lapsed while someone is on a roundabout being marked, a naive gate revokes the route out from under them and bins the run.

Fix Entitlement is read once, when a drive starts, and never re-checked until it ends. An unresolved status falls back to the device's last known answer, and only a device that has never resolved one at all counts as lapsed — which is harmless, since it has no cached route details either. Losing a few pence to a grace case is nothing against destroying a learner's mock test, and the result still writes through the existing progress outbox either way.

Before submission

What App Review will look for

  • Restore purchases reachable without an account action — on the sheet and in Settings.
  • Prices read from StoreKit, never the £3 and £9 hard-coded in the app. Storefronts differ.
  • Terms and Privacy linked from the paywall itself. Both already exist in docs/ and on the site.
  • Renewal terms stated next to the button: what it costs, how often, that it auto-renews, how to stop it.
  • The sheet is dismissible at any time, with no timed delay on the close control.
  • Account deletion still works with an active subscription — and says plainly that deleting doesn't cancel billing.
  • Sandbox-tested end to end: buy, restore on a second device, cancel, let it lapse, resubscribe.

What I'd want your call on

The free route per centre is more generous than it sounds, and that's fine. Almost everyone only ever cares about one centre, so in practice it's one free route — but it reads as an offer rather than a restriction, and it costs nothing to say it the generous way.

No free trial is proposed. At £3 a week, the weekly product already is the trial, and it collects a card instead of a cancellation reminder. Worth revisiting if conversion disappoints, but starting without one keeps phase four much simpler.

If this looks right, the next step is the written spec, then the implementation plan — no code until you've seen both.
